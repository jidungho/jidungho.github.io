York Yang of DYNA Robotics defines a bubble as the gap between current capability and human expectations, multiplied by time — so the real question is not whether robotics is overhyped, but whether today's robots can produce commercial value fast enough to justify the capital. By that test much of the market qualifies: annual funding tripled from $2.86B to $8.76B across 235 deals between 2022 and 2025, yet Tesla acknowledged in January 2026 that no Optimus robot was doing useful factory work, and Unitree's IPO prospectus put only ~9% of humanoid revenue in true industrial deployment — mostly reception and tour-guide duty, roughly $2M of real manufacturing revenue from the world's largest humanoid shipper. His diagnosis is that the field imported the wrong analogy: robots have no installed base and no distribution layer the way cars or browsers did, shipping hardware is not a channel (the test is whether the next deployment is faster than the last), and pre-training scale without a post-training loop fed by real deployments does not compound. DYNA's own longest deployment has run about ten months of daily use, but getting there still took weeks-to-months of on-site engineering that did not transfer cleanly to the next customer — which is why they bet on end-to-end integration instead of model-first or hardware-first. The bubble, he concludes, is the assumption that capability turns into commercial value on an LLM-shaped curve.
